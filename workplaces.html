Not enough discipline rest

Almost everyone at work notices it in themselves: the phone picked up without ever deciding to, the full day that feels like nothing got done, the weekend that disappears. Most people quietly blame their own discipline. This workshop helps employees see what’s really happening to their attention, and why their minds so rarely get to rest.

What you hear at work, and what’s underneath

These come up over lunch or in passing, usually with a laugh, and almost never go any further. Each sounds like a small personal complaint. Heard together, they point to the same thing.

There’s so much to do, and I feel like I’m achieving nothing.

Not a lack of effort. Constant switching.

Microsoft’s 2025 Work Trend Index found that people are interrupted roughly every two minutes during the workday, by meetings, emails and notifications. Research by Gloria Mark at UC Irvine found that getting fully back to a task after an interruption can take around 25 minutes.

A day spent switching feels busy and draining at the same time, and still leaves the sense that nothing got finished.

I don’t have time for this. I’m too busy.

Every gap is already full.

The commute, the lift, the lunch break, the minute before a meeting starts: almost every empty moment in the day now has a screen in it.

Those moments used to be when the mind wandered, sorted through the day and came up with ideas. It’s why good thoughts still arrive in the shower, one of the few places the phone can’t follow.

Where did the weekend go? I couldn’t even rest properly.

Scrolling isn’t rest.

A scroll feels like a break, but the brain keeps taking in something new every few seconds. People come back from it more tired, not less.

Real rest is time with no input at all, and it has quietly disappeared from most weekends.

I was up till two scrolling again.

Taking the day back, at night.

When the day belongs to work, the hours before sleep feel like the only ones that are ours. So we stretch them, one more video at a time. It even has a name: revenge bedtime procrastination.

The cost shows up the next morning, and the cycle starts again.

I feel burnt out.

A mind that never gets switched off.

Burnout has many causes, and this workshop isn’t a treatment for it. But a mind that is pulled at all day, rests in front of a screen and sleeps late is running without a break.

Seeing that clearly is often the first step people take towards feeling better.

Sources: Microsoft Work Trend Index 2025; Gloria Mark, Attention Span (2023).

Why it never reaches HR

At school, parents bring this problem to teachers. At work, nobody brings it to anyone.

Because it feels personal, people try to fix it alone, with app limits, deleted apps or a “digital detox” that lasts a few days. When the old patterns return, they feel a little worse about themselves, and say even less.

So it rarely comes up in one-on-ones. But it sits underneath a lot of what does: tiredness that sleep doesn’t fix, stress, and the feeling of always being on.

The pull isn’t a personal failing. The apps on our phones are built by teams whose whole job is to keep us engaged. Helping employees see that is a way to support their wellbeing and mental health on something nearly everyone experiences and few ever raise.

A workshop people take part in, not one they sit through

There’s no lecture and no list of rules. Employees are guided step by step through games and activities that let them see the game for themselves, and then notice how it plays out in their own day.

  1. Take the blame off

    We start with what most people have never been told: this isn’t a lack of discipline. They find out how free apps make money, and why their attention is what’s being sold.

  2. See how the game works

    A mystery-chit game shows why the next swipe always feels like it might be the good one. Then teams design an app built to keep people hooked, and discover they already know every trick.

  3. Feel what switching costs

    A two-minute pen-and-paper test lets everyone feel how much slower the brain gets when it keeps jumping between two things. People who multitask all day are often the most surprised.

  4. Name the pull

    Every time someone reaches for the phone, there’s a reason behind it. Participants map their own workday moments to these reasons.

    Select one to see what it looks like.

  5. Notice, don’t fix

    Instead of a list of tips, people leave with a small experiment for the week: one meeting with phones away, one commute without the phone, and the habit of catching the reach before the phone is in their hand.

Not anti-phone
Phones aren’t the enemy, and nobody is asked to give theirs up.
No lecture, no blame
Observations rather than instructions, shared in a way people can recognise in themselves.
Private by default
Nobody shares their screen time, and nothing individual is reported back to managers or HR.
A session of its own
It runs as a standalone, in-person session of about an hour, not squeezed into another event.

Who runs the workshop

Puney Chawla

B.E., BITS Pilani

Puney on LinkedIn

I studied engineering at BITS Pilani, and I run these workshops because I’ve felt the same pull myself. I’ve uninstalled apps and reinstalled them within days, and kept my phone close in case someone important called. Nobody did.

On a recent flight, with no internet and nothing downloaded, I realised I couldn’t sit with my own thoughts for half an hour. That moment shaped this workshop. Each session is built from research on attention, from what I’ve noticed in myself, and from what people share in the room.
