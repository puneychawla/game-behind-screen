Phone addiction habits, built by design

When students can’t put their phones down, the word everyone reaches for is addiction. But the apps on those phones are built by teams whose whole job is to keep them scrolling. This workshop starts by taking that blame off, so students can see what’s happening to their attention, and why.

What you hear, and what’s really going on

Close to nine in ten Indian teenagers aged 14 to 16 have a smartphone at home, and about three in four use one for social media. Teachers hear the same complaints from parents and students every week, and each one quietly ends with the same verdict. Here’s what sits underneath.

He’s always on his phone. He’s addicted.

The app was built for “always.”

Most apps are free because they earn from advertising, so every extra minute a student spends on them is revenue. Endless feeds, autoplay, streaks and red notification dots are designed so there’s never a natural place to stop.

On one side is a 15-year-old at 11 at night with a test the next day. On the other is a team of engineers. It was never a fair fight.

She studies for hours, but nothing stays.

Not weak memory. Constant switching.

The brain doesn’t multitask; it switches, and every switch costs something. Research by Gloria Mark at UC Irvine found that attention on a single screen now lasts about 47 seconds on average, and getting fully back to a task after an interruption can take around 25 minutes.

What never gets properly stored can’t be recalled in the exam hall.

He just can’t focus anymore.

Focus isn’t a trait. It’s a condition.

The same student who can’t study for twenty minutes on a Sunday will study for five hours the night before an exam. Same brain, same phone. What changed is the setting: a visible finish line, a clear reward, and no shortcut left.

Once students understand those conditions, they can start to recreate them.

We took the phone away. It didn’t help.

Bans don’t build understanding.

Most students have already tried. They’ve uninstalled Instagram, deactivated accounts and set app locks, then reinstalled within a week. Being told no often makes the phone more attractive, and the arguments turn it into a battleground at home.

What’s missing isn’t willpower. It’s an understanding of why the pull keeps winning.

Sources: Annual Status of Education Report (ASER) 2024, Pratham; Gloria Mark, Attention Span (2023).

A workshop students take part in, not one they sit through

There’s no lecture and no list of rules. Students are guided step by step through games and activities that let them see the game for themselves, and then notice how it plays out in their own day.

  1. Take the blame off

    We start by telling students what few people have: this isn’t their fault. They find out how free apps make money, and why their time is what’s being sold.

  2. See how the game works

    A mystery-chit game shows why the next swipe always feels like it might be the good one. Then teams design their own app to keep students hooked, and discover they already know every trick.

  3. Feel what switching costs

    A two-minute pen-and-paper test lets students feel how much slower the brain gets when it keeps jumping between two things. Most leave seeing their “weak memory” differently.

  4. Name the urge

    Every time a student picks up the phone, there’s a reason behind it. Students map their own moments to these reasons.

    Select one to see what it looks like.

  5. Pause and notice

    Instead of a fix, students leave with a small experiment for the week: catch the reach before the phone is in their hand, name the reason, and come back to what they were doing.

Not anti-phone
Phones aren’t the enemy, and students are never asked to give them up.
Friendly and free of blame
Run by a facilitator close enough in age to talk like a friend, sharing observations rather than instructions.
No arguments at home
Students are asked to keep what they learn for themselves, not to use it against their parents.
Built in two parts
The first session shows how the game works. The second helps students understand their own urges.

Each session runs for about an hour. All it needs is a projector and a little room for group activities.

Who it’s for

The workshop is run at institutes for students aged 14 to 20, and is built around two groups.

Students who got their phone in the last year or so

Habits of attention are still forming. A first phone is when those habits set, which makes it the best time to make them visible.

Students working towards a goal

Board exams, JEE, NEET and other entrance exams ask for long stretches of unbroken focus. These are the students who feel the cost of switching most.

Past workshops

8 workshops so far, and more than 300 students who’ve taken part.

They’ve been run at these institutes in Delhi NCR and Bangalore, with students from class 9 to the first year of engineering.

I studied engineering at BITS Pilani, and I run these workshops because I’ve felt the same pull myself. I’ve uninstalled apps and reinstalled them within days, and kept my phone close in case someone important called. Nobody did.

I’m close enough in age to the students to talk with them as a friend rather than lecture them. Each workshop is built from research on attention, from what I’ve noticed in myself, and from what students share in the room.
